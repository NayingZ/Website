Research & Grant

My work centers on the germline genetic basis of childhood and early-onset cancers. Across projects, a common thread: using population-scale genomic data — dried blood spots, genotyping arrays, sequencing — to identify heritable susceptibility, and to do so in ancestrally diverse populations where such risk has been least well characterized.

2025 – Present Molecular & Genetic Epidemiology — Cross-Laboratory Collaboration
Interdisciplinary Research Collaborator · USC
Collaborate across USC laboratories on projects spanning wet-lab science, molecular epidemiology, and computational genomics. This work took me beyond computational research into hands-on laboratory technique, including DNA extraction and PCR analysis. Parallel cross-lab projects integrate albumin adduct profiling with genotype data, and use Multiethnic Cohort genotypes to develop models predicting GSTM1/GSTT1 deletion status — enabling gene–environment interaction analyses in cohorts that lack direct deletion typing.
2024 – Present Whole-Exome Sequencing of California Newborn Dried Blood Spots
Graduate Research Assistant
Lead end-to-end WES pipelines — from raw sequence processing and alignment through SNV, indel, and CNV calling, QC, and annotation — for germline studies using dried blood spots collected at birth through the California biobank. Analyses span four pediatric cancer cohorts: 320 individuals with glioma, 50 with atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor (ATRT), 22 with medulloblastoma, and 114 samples from families with multiple cancer-affected members, characterizing cancer predisposition variants underlying early-onset CNS tumors and familial cancer clustering.
2025 WES Analysis of Early-Onset Soft Tissue Sarcoma in California
Multiethnic family-based population study
Led whole-exome sequencing analyses identifying germline cancer predisposition variants in early-onset soft tissue sarcoma patients and their affected siblings. Characterized candidate susceptibility loci with ancestry-specific patterns, and identified a novel recurrent USP6 mutation in two unrelated Southeast Asian individuals (Filipino and Guamanian) with rhabdomyosarcoma — a potential ancestry-associated susceptibility signal warranting further investigation.

Relationship between genetically determined telomere length and childhood glioma risk
Zhou N, Li S, Foss-Skiftesvik J, Dahlin AM, Bybjerg-Grauholm J, Melin B, de Smith AJ, Walsh KM, Wiemels JL. Acta Neuropathologica Communications. 2026;14(1):93. PMID: 41668119. doi:10.1186/s40478-025-02199-2

Key Findings

  • First genetic evidence linking longer genetically predicted leukocyte telomere length (LTL) to increased pediatric glioma risk, using Mendelian randomization and polygenic risk scores across 4,069 cases and 8,778 controls from the largest available childhood glioma meta-GWAS.
  • Each 1-SD increase in genetically predicted LTL roughly doubled childhood glioma risk: OR 2.12 (95% CI 1.32–3.39, P = .002) in the multi-ancestry group and OR 2.16 (95% CI 1.16–4.03, P = .015) in the European group.
  • The contributing variants differed from those driving adult glioma — rs59294613 (POT1), rs8105767 (ZNF208), and rs7705526 (TERT) in children, versus TERT and RTEL1 in adults, with RTEL1 showing a null pediatric effect.
  • The effect is age-dependent: OR 1.89 (95% CI 1.33–2.69, P < .001) in children diagnosed after age 6 versus OR 1.04 (95% CI 0.74–1.45, P = .73) at or before age 6 (Phet = .006). A generalized additive model showed LTL PRS rising steadily with diagnosis age from 0–10 years, then plateauing.
