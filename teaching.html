Teaching & Service

I teach epidemiology to students who arrive from very different starting points — some with clinical training, some with none at all. My aim is to scaffold deliberately, so that the biology and the quantitative reasoning reinforce each other rather than compete for attention.

Courses Taught

2025 – 2026 PM 527 — Epidemiology of Infectious Diseases
Graduate Course Instructor · Keck School of Medicine of USC · Syllabus (PDF)
  • 4units
  • 15weeks
  • 3.74/4.00student evaluation (report)
  • 77%evaluation response
Details
  • Instructor of record for this 4-unit, 15-week graduate course from November 2025 to May 2026, teaching 26 MPH and graduate students who arrived from very different scientific backgrounds. I set the learning objectives, built the syllabus and assessments, delivered the live sessions and assigned the final grades.
  • The first problem was diagnostic rather than pedagogical: gaps in pathogen biology and immunology, not in epidemiology, were what held some students back. Rather than teach pathogens as isolated facts, I built the course around core biologic principles that transfer across diseases, then organized disease epidemiology by route of transmission. Weeks 1 to 4 cover measures of disease frequency, clinical syndromes, study design, the cast of bacterial, fungal, viral and parasitic pathogens, and immunology. Weeks 5 to 12 move through respiratory, fecal-oral, direct and indirect contact, sexual and intravascular transmission, then vector-borne, zoonotic and environmental exposure. Weeks 13 to 15 close with vaccinology and antibiotic resistance, outbreak investigation and disease control.
  • The course runs flipped: recorded lectures, readings and videos before class, with live sessions reserved for case discussion and problem solving. Tiered case-based poll questions and immediate in-class practice surfaced misconceptions early enough to act on them, and I invited subject-matter experts as guest lecturers to deepen individual topics.
  • Assessment was deliberately multi-modal so that no single format decided a grade: weekly quizzes (10%), individual applied assignments on HIV screening, influenza, hepatitis, West Nile virus and CDC case studies (15%), group article reviews (5%), group discussion and public service announcement projects (15%), midterm (15%), final exam (25%) and participation anchored by a CDC outbreak investigation exercise (15%).
  • Across the 20 students who responded (77% response rate), evaluations averaged 3.74/4.00, with Course Design and Instructional Practices highest at 3.77 and Course Impact at 3.74. The strongest individual items were carefully explaining difficult concepts, alignment of assignments with objectives, and grading that reflects the quality of work, each at 3.80/4.00.
  • What students wrote matters more to me than the numbers. One called the course "a joy to be in. Great class structure and assignments to ensure understanding." Another wrote that I "explained difficult concepts in an easy manner and in a digestible way," and that my "care for her students also came across in lectures and assignments." A third wrote, "Dr. Zhou has been one of the best instructors that I have had at USC."
  • Mid-semester feedback said the inherited material was broad and the volume felt heavy, so I narrowed each unit to explicit focus areas and added practice questions. A student described the change directly: I "did a great job adapting and evolving the course to be more focused," and "began clarifying the main topics and focus areas for each section, which really improved the course." Teaching a dense subject to a mixed-background audience, then adjusting in real time when it is not landing, is the same skill I will need explaining a genetic diagnosis to a family.
