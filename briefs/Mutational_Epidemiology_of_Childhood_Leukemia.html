Investigating the causes of childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia through mutational epidemiology

Adam J. de Smith, Naying Zhou, Yunqi Li, Tanxin Liu, Nathan Patrus, Joseph L. Wiemels

A review of mutational signature analysis as a route to identifying environmental causes of childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia. It summarizes the signatures reported to date, the established and emerging risk factors, and the study designs that would link signatures to measured exposures.

Rationale

Limitations of questionnaire-based exposure assessment

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia is the most common cancer in children, with incidence peaking between two and five years of age. Most epidemiologic evidence on its causes derives from case-control studies using parental recall of exposures.

This approach has two principal limitations: questionnaire data are subject to recall bias, and an observed association provides little information about the mechanism by which an exposure might be leukemogenic. Geospatial studies, which link a birth address to an exposure database, avoid recall bias but substitute residence for dose.

Mutagenic processes damage DNA in characteristic ways, and the resulting patterns — mutational signatures — can be recovered from sequenced tumors. In adult cancer this has identified exposures that questionnaires could not: aristolochic acid signatures concentrated in kidney cancers from Romania, Serbia and Thailand; colibactin signatures enriched in high-incidence and early-onset colorectal cancer; and a mutagenic contribution from air pollution in lung cancer among never-smokers.

The principal obstacle is that the cohorts holding tumor sequencing data lack exposure information, and the studies holding exposure data lack sequencing. The two have not yet been combined in a childhood cancer.

Natural history

Timing of leukemogenic events

The initiating lesion in childhood ALL can be dated to fetal development, which defines the window any preventive intervention would have to reach.

Two lines of evidence support this. Monozygotic twins concordant for ALL carry chromosomal translocations with identical breakpoints, presumed to have transferred between twins through a shared placenta. Backtracking studies detect the same leukemia-driving fusions in neonatal blood spots from children diagnosed years later.

An initiating lesion is not sufficient for leukemia. Second-hit alterations, including deletions and point mutations, occur closer to overt disease, so postnatal exposures are also relevant. Both windows are early.

The mutations themselves carry timing information. V(D)J recombination features at immunoglobulin heavy and light chains in common childhood ALL show gene-segment usage characteristic of fetal development, coinciding with the timing of ETV6::RUNX1 translocations. TCF3::PBX1 translocations and transcription factor deletions instead bear marks of later-acting adaptive immune enzymes, including terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase and activation-induced cytidine deaminase. Infant ALL shows a more mature immunoglobulin rearrangement, suggesting a clone of later developmental origin.

This distinguishes leukemia from solid tumors as a subject of mutational analysis. The hematopoietic system replaces approximately 1011 cells per day, and physiological genome-editing enzymes are active in the lineage that becomes malignant, so some signatures reflect endogenous immune processes rather than environmental exposure.

Mutational signatures

Signatures reported in childhood ALL

Sequencing studies of childhood ALL have catalogued the signatures present. Excluding the clock-like signatures common to all tumor types, three recur and each corresponds to a candidate exposure.

APOBEC signatures (SBS2 and SBS13) reflect cytidine deaminase activity and are enriched in ETV6::RUNX1 fusion B-ALL. SBS18 is attributed to oxidative damage from reactive oxygen species and has been associated with air pollutants and pesticides; it is most prominent in LMO1/2 T-ALL and PAX5alt B-ALL. SBS7, the ultraviolet signature, is concentrated in the aneuploid subtypes: hyperdiploid, near-haploid and iAMP21.

The clinical cohorts that produced these frequencies did not collect epidemiologic or environmental exposure information, so direct exposure-to-signature analysis has not been possible. These distributions are therefore hypothesis-generating rather than causal evidence.

Risk factors

Established, emerging and inconsistent risk factors

The review grades the evidence rather than presenting it uniformly. Some associations are established, others emerging, and several widely discussed exposures remain inconsistent. This classification determines which exposures merit signature-based investigation.

Proposed study designs

Linking signatures to measured exposures

The review sets out two study designs, differing in cost and feasibility.

The first is to link existing mutational signature results from cohorts that have already whole-genome sequenced large numbers of childhood ALL patients to exposure data already collected or feasibly collectable. The second is to add paired tumor-normal sequencing to case-control studies that hold detailed environmental exposure assessment; the Childhood Cancer and Leukemia International Consortium is identified as a suitable setting.

The stated priorities are the same in both designs: determine the etiologies of the signatures observed (APOBEC, reactive oxygen species, ultraviolet), conduct an epidemiologic study of RAG recombination-mediated deletions, and stratify by molecular subtype, which will require large samples or targeted enrollment of specific subtypes.

Open questions

Why children of Hispanic/Latino ethnicity have the highest and most rapidly rising ALL incidence in the United States. Risk alleles at ARID5B, GATA3 and IKZF1 are more common in these populations but do not fully account for the difference.

Why incidence is higher in males. Sex differences in mutational signatures have been demonstrated in adult cancers but not examined in childhood ALL.

Whether global variation in ALL incidence has a mutational correlate, which would require sequencing patients outside the United States and Europe.

Precedents in adult cancer

More than 5,000 tumors across smoking-associated cancer types: reported smoking correlated with mutation burden and with the tobacco signature SBS4.

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma across eight countries: APOBEC mutagenesis was a major contributor, although no signature explained global variation in incidence.

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma: aristolochic acid signatures concentrated in Romania, Serbia and Thailand, and a signature of unknown cause common in Japan and rare elsewhere.

Colorectal cancer across eleven countries: colibactin signatures enriched in high-incidence regions and in early-onset cases.

These methods were developed for adult, carcinogen-driven cancers. The review argues that they transfer to childhood leukemia, and that applying them is now a matter of assembling a cohort with both sequencing and exposure data rather than of methodological development.
