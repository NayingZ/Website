Relationship between genetically determined telomere length and childhood glioma risk

Naying Zhou, Shaobo Li, Jon Foss-Skiftesvik, Anna M. Dahlin, Jonas Bybjerg-Grauholm, Beatrice Melin, Adam J. de Smith, Kyle M. Walsh, Joseph L. Wiemels

Mendelian randomization in 4,069 childhood glioma cases and 8,778 controls tested whether genetically predicted leukocyte telomere length is associated with glioma risk. Longer predicted telomere length was associated with higher risk, and the association was confined to children diagnosed after age six.

Mendelian randomization

Association with glioma risk, by age at diagnosis

Genetically predicted longer leukocyte telomere length is an established risk factor for adult glioma. This study tested the same relationship in children and found that it holds, but that it is restricted to children diagnosed after age six.

Telomeres are repeated sequences that cap chromosomes and shorten with each cell division. Short telomeres are generally associated with genomic instability, but glioma shows the opposite relationship: alleles associated with longer telomeres are associated with higher risk, consistent with greater replicative capacity permitting the accumulation of oncogenic alterations.

Measured telomere length is difficult to use as an exposure in children. Pre-diagnostic measurement is rarely available, and post-diagnostic measurement reflects disease and treatment as well as baseline risk. Mendelian randomization substitutes inherited variants for the measured exposure; because alleles are fixed at conception, they are not influenced by adiposity, treatment or the tumor itself.

Inverse-variance weighted, weighted median and weighted mode estimates were concordant in both the multi-ancestry and European analyses, and leave-one-out analysis indicated that no single variant drove the result. The MR-Egger intercept gave no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy, and MR-Clust identified a single non-null cluster, consistent with the variants acting through one shared pathway.

Single-variant analysis

Contributing variants differ from those in adult glioma

Applying the same 15-variant instrument in childhood and adult glioma produced different rankings of individual variant contributions, indicating that the shared telomere association does not rest on the same variants.

POT1 encodes a component of the shelterin complex; cancer-predisposing variants in POT1 are typically associated with longer but structurally fragile telomeres. rs59294613 (POT1) was the strongest single contributor in children and was not a leading variant in adults.

rs8105767 (ZNF208) was the second contributor in children and is not an established adult glioma risk variant. It has been reported in association with favorable prognosis in low-grade glioma, the predominant histology in children.

Conversely, rs34978822 (RTEL1) and rs75691080 (RTEL1/STMN3) ranked second and third in adults and were null in children. rs7705526 (TERT) contributed in both populations, but the childhood effect was significantly smaller (Wald test, P < .001), consistent with reports that TERT promoter mutations are uncommon in pediatric nervous-system tumors and occur mainly in older children.

No individual variant reached genome-wide significance in the childhood glioma GWAS, as expected at this sample size. The instrument aggregates these individually small effects into a single testable exposure.

Polygenic risk score

Polygenic risk score and age at diagnosis

An individual-level telomere-length polygenic risk score was computed in the U.S. cohort. Among cases, the score increased continuously with age at diagnosis up to age ten, indicating a gradient rather than a threshold at age six.

Among cases diagnosed before age 10, each additional year of age at diagnosis was associated with a higher telomere-length polygenic score (β = 0.100 per year, 95% CI 0.052–0.149, P < .001, n = 1,588). Beyond age 10 the relationship was flat (β = 0.007, P = .70, n = 709). The pattern was present in the European and Latino subgroups and absent in the smaller Asian and African American strata.

Two interpretations are consistent with this pattern. First, telomere biology may contribute more to gliomas arising later in childhood, with earlier tumors driven by other mechanisms. Second, the instrument was derived in adults, and telomere dynamics in early life differ from adult dynamics: longitudinal data show rapid shortening from birth to age three, slower decline to age five, and stabilization by about age nine.

In either case, an adult-derived genetic instrument applied to children may yield an age-conditional effect that appears null in the youngest stratum.

Glioma subtypes

Effect estimates were above one across most glioma subtypes, and larger for low-grade than for high-grade tumors. In the polygenic score analysis only astrocytoma reached significance (OR 1.40 per unit of score, 95% CI 1.03–1.89, P = .03).

The high-grade, glioblastoma and oligodendroglioma subgroups are small — the cohort contains 84 glioblastomas — so null results in these strata reflect limited power.

Confounding by adiposity

Obesity shortens measured telomere length and has been inconsistently associated with glioma, making it a plausible confounder in observational studies of telomere length.

Here the exposure is derived from genotype measured in neonatal dried blood spots using an instrument from adult telomere GWAS, so body weight at diagnosis cannot account for the association.
