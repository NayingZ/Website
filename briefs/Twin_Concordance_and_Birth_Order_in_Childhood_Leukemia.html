A first-born twin has a higher risk of acute leukemia in a population-based assessment of cancer in twins in California, and a lower than anticipated rate of twin concordance

Eric M. Nickels, Naying Zhou, Joseph L. Wiemels

Linkage of the California Birth Master Statistical File to the California Cancer Registry for 1982–2022 identified 255 twin pairs with at least one leukemia diagnosis. Concordance was substantially lower than estimates from recruited twin cohorts, and within discordant pairs the affected child was more often the first-born twin.

Concordance

Twin concordance in an unselected population

Published estimates of twin concordance for acute lymphoblastic leukemia, derived from recruited twin cohorts, range from approximately 5% to 25%. In this unselected population-based cohort, observed concordance for leukemia was 0.8%.

The difference is consistent with ascertainment bias. Recruited cohorts depend on voluntary enrollment, and families in which both twins are affected are more likely to be identified and reported, which inflates the concordance estimate those studies are designed to produce.

This analysis linked the California Birth Master Statistical File to the California Cancer Registry and identified every twin pair in which at least one sibling was diagnosed with leukemia between 1982 and 2022. Because accrual is population-based, concordant pairs are no more likely to be identified than discordant ones.

Two of 255 pairs were concordant. Adjusting for the estimated proportion of monozygotic twins raises the lymphoid estimate to 1.4%. Further accounting for the approximately 70% of monozygotic twins that are monochorionic — and therefore share the placental circulation through which a leukemic clone is thought to transfer in utero — gives 2.4% overall and 3.4% in the 1–10 year age group.

Prior estimates place concordance for leukemia diagnosed in the first year of life near 100%. In this cohort, none of the six lymphoid leukemias diagnosed before age one had an affected co-twin.

Twin studies established that the initiating translocation in childhood leukemia can arise in utero and transfer between siblings through a shared placenta. These results do not contradict that, but the low concordance indicates that post-initiation events — acquired mutations, epigenetic change and discordant environmental exposure — contribute more to etiology than previously assumed.

The single concordant lymphoid pair had different diagnoses: Burkitt cell leukemia in one sibling and B lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma in the other. These entities do not share known driver mutations, making shared fetal circulation an unlikely explanation; shared germline predisposition to hematologic malignancy is more plausible but cannot be tested in registry data.

Most prior population-based twin studies were conducted in non-Latino White populations. The California birth cohort is multiethnic and majority Latino, the group with the highest and most rapidly rising incidence of childhood ALL in the United States.

Birth order

Plurality order and leukemia risk in discordant pairs

Among pairs discordant for leukemia, the affected child was more often the first-born twin (OR 1.80 for all leukemias, P = .001; OR 2.26 for lymphoblastic leukemia, P < .001). This association has not previously been reported.

The direction of effect reversed for acute myeloid leukemia (OR 0.55, P = .127). The same asymmetry is observed in singletons, in whom first-born children have elevated ALL risk and reduced AML risk.

Plurality order is unlikely to be causal in itself. The analysis therefore examined DNA methylation at birth for differences associated with delivery order.

Two targeted analyses were performed. Of 341 CpG sites previously associated with birth order across successive gestations, 305 were present on the array; mean delta-beta values (first-born minus second-born methylation) were significantly lower in pairs where the case was the first-born twin (Wilcoxon rank-sum P = .037; 24 versus 17 pairs). Of 22,100 CpG sites nominally associated with cortisol, 19,193 were present on the array, and the same comparison was significant (P = .011).

These results support a testable hypothesis: greater early cortisol exposure is thought to reduce ALL risk, and the cortisol surge accompanying labor is a major early exposure. This is consistent with the observation that scheduled pre-labor cesarean delivery has been associated with increased ALL risk whereas emergency cesarean, which follows labor, has not.

Limitations

The registry contains no zygosity data, so monozygosity was estimated from the sex distribution rather than measured; in a subset genotyped on a SNP array, approximately half of same-sex pairs were monozygotic, consistent with the estimate. Cancer data are right-censored at 2022, so twins born in later years have had less follow-up time; four decades of accrual limits but does not remove this effect.

The methylation analysis is based on 41 pairs and requires replication in a larger twin cohort.

Clinical implication

These results do not alter clinical management. They do affect counseling: when one twin is diagnosed with ALL, the concordance risk quoted to the family should reflect an unselected population rather than a recruited cohort.
